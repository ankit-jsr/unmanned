## Day 2: grant writers

The most expensive grant proposal is the one you never had a chance of winning.

Before drafting, score three things honestly:

1. Mission alignment: does your work *directly* match the funder's stated priorities?
2. Eligibility: every hard criterion met? If any is unconfirmed, stop and check.
3. Capacity: have you delivered this kind of work at this scale?

All strong: write it. Any weak: let it go, and write down why in one sentence.

I wrote the full routine up, with a worked example, and made it a free Claude skill.

## Day 3: a failure I'm publishing

I caught one of my own products making things up.

My free skill for academic reviewer response letters passed its tests 33 out of 33. Then a
review found that in one test it had invented methods details the author never gave it: a
matching procedure and a supplementary table that didn't exist. For a letter that goes to a
journal, that's the worst possible failure.

The tests rewarded specifics, so the made-up ones passed.

The fix: anything the author hasn't told it now becomes a visible [placeholder]. I re-ran
every test with fresh agents and a separate grader. New score: 39 of 40. The one remaining
failure is published alongside the results.

A lower honest score beats a perfect wrong one.

## Day 4: researchers

A response to reviewers has one job: make it easy to see what happened to every comment.

- Decide accept / partial / push back *before* drafting.
- Number everything. Split bundled comments.
- Quote, acknowledge, say what you changed, say where.
- Push back with evidence, not adjectives.
- Never write a change you didn't make.

## Day 5: newsletter operators

If an issue is hard to write, the problem is usually upstream of the writing. You have a topic,
not a point.

"Sponsorship pricing" is a topic.
"Small newsletters undercharge because they price on CPM instead of what their list delivers"
is a point.

My one-page plan: claim → three hooks → three sections with word budgets → one CTA → three
subject lines. Then write.
