# Should you even apply? A grant fit check you can run before writing a word

The most expensive grant proposal is the one you never had a real chance of winning. It costs
the same days of writing as a winning one, and it teaches you less, because the debrief (if you
get one) arrives months later and rarely says "you were never a fit."

Most grant writers know this. Fewer have a routine for catching bad fits early, because the
moment a promising RFP lands is exactly when there's pressure to start drafting. This is a
routine you can run in one sitting, before any drafting, with nothing but the RFP and an honest
description of your organisation.

## Step 1: Write down what the funder actually wants, in their words

Pull three to five priorities straight from the RFP. Quote them; don't paraphrase. Paraphrasing
is where wishful thinking creeps in. "Expanding access to stable housing" quietly becomes "anything
that helps people who might lose their housing," and suddenly your workforce programme looks like
a fit.

If the RFP lists scoring criteria, start there. They tell you what reviewers will be told to
reward.

## Step 2: Score three dimensions, honestly

Give each one of three grades: **Strong**, **Partial** or **Weak**. Add one line saying why.

**Mission alignment.** Does your work directly match what the funder said it cares about? Not
"could be framed as." Directly. A useful test: if a programme officer read your mission statement
cold, would they see their priority in it?

**Eligibility.** Does your organisation meet every hard criterion? Geography, organisation type,
budget range, years operating, restrictions on prior funding. This one is binary in practice. If any
criterion is unconfirmed, it is not Strong. Call the funder or check the FAQ before you go further.

**Capacity.** Do you have the track record and staff to deliver what this funder expects at this
award size? A pilot workshop is not a track record for a three-year programme grant. Be specific
about what you've actually delivered.

## Step 3: Turn the scores into a verdict

- **High:** all three Strong, or at most one Partial.
- **Medium:** one or two Partial, none Weak.
- **Low:** anything Weak, or eligibility still uncertain.

The rule that matters most: **never give yourself a High while any eligibility criterion is
unconfirmed.** That's how organisations spend weeks on proposals that get screened out before a
reviewer reads them.

## Step 4: Do the thing the verdict tells you to do

**If it's High,** don't relax. List the three RFP requirements most likely to be thin in your
first draft. These are not the obvious ones you'll cover anyway, but the ones that quietly sink
funded-looking proposals: evaluation plans, sustainability after the grant ends, letters of
support you haven't asked for yet.

**If it's Medium,** list two or three concrete actions that would move a Partial to a Strong
before the deadline. A partner letter. Outcome data you have but haven't pulled. A conversation
with the programme officer. If you can't name actions that fit the timeline, treat it as a Low.

**If it's Low,** write down the blocker in one plain sentence and move on. "The funder requires
three years of direct housing services; we have none." Saying it plainly is what makes it easy to
let go.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

A workforce development organisation in Denver, seven years old with a modest budget, finds a
housing stability fund for the metro area.

| Dimension | Score | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mission alignment | Partial | Clients face housing risk, but the mission is employment |
| Eligibility | Strong | Geography, budget and years operating all match |
| Capacity | Partial | One pilot financial coaching workshop; funder expects housing programming |

**Verdict: Medium.** Before applying, they'd want a letter of support from a housing partner and
client data showing housing outcomes. If they can get both before the deadline, it's worth
writing. If not, it isn't.

## What this check is not

It is not a probability of winning. Nobody can give you a percentage that means anything. It is
also not a substitute for knowing the funder. Relationships still matter. It's a way to make the
go/no-go call deliberately instead of by momentum.
