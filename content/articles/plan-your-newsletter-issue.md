# Plan the issue before you write it: a one-page newsletter plan

When an issue is hard to write, the problem is usually upstream of the writing. You have a topic
but not a point. "AI in the workplace" is a topic. "Most teams adopting AI tools are measuring
the wrong thing" is a point. You can write the second one. The first one produces a blank page.

This is the one-page plan I use to get from topic to point before drafting.

## 1. Sharpen the topic into a claim someone could disagree with

Write your topic, then keep narrowing until it's a claim a reasonable reader could argue with.

- Topic: *sponsorship pricing*
- Narrower: *small newsletters price sponsorships badly*
- Claim: *small newsletters undercharge because they price on CPM instead of on what their
  sponsors actually get from a small, trusted list*

If nobody could disagree with it, it isn't a claim yet. If you can't support it, it's the wrong
claim. Narrow somewhere else.

## 2. Write three hooks, one of each type

Your headline and opening line do one job: make the right reader keep going. Write one of each:

- **Direct:** say the point. *You're pricing sponsorships on the wrong metric.*
- **Curiosity:** open a gap the issue closes. *Why can a 1,000-subscriber newsletter charge more
  than one with 50,000?*
- **Contrarian:** name the belief you're challenging. *CPM is a TV metric. Stop using it.*

Pick the one that fits your list. You'll often use another as a subject line.

## 3. Fix the structure: three sections, with a word budget

Three sections is enough for most issues:

1. **The problem**: what's going wrong and who it's happening to.
2. **The better frame**: your claim, with the reasoning or example that supports it.
3. **What to do**: the concrete change a reader can make this week.

Give each section a rough word budget before you write. It stops section one from eating the issue.

## 4. Choose exactly one call to action

One. If you ask readers to reply, share, buy and follow, they'll do none of them. For small lists,
a low-commitment ask like "hit reply and tell me how you price" usually beats a link. It
also tells you what to write next.

## 5. Draft three subject lines

Write one direct, one curiosity and one contrarian, the same as the hooks. Test them over a few
issues and you'll learn what your list opens.

## The whole plan, on one page

| Part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | Small newsletters undercharge because they price on CPM, not on what their list delivers |
| Hook | You're pricing sponsorships on the wrong metric |
| Structure | The CPM trap (200) / Price on outcomes (300) / How to reprice (200) |
| CTA | Hit reply with how you price today |
| Subject lines | How to reprice your sponsorships / The number sponsors actually track / Industry rates are a trap for small lists |

Once that's written, the draft is mostly filling in boxes.
