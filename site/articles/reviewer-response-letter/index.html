How to write a response to reviewers that's easy to say yes to

A response letter has one job: make it easy for the editor and reviewers to see that every concern was taken seriously, and exactly what happened to each one. Most letters fail not because the revisions were weak, but because the letter makes reviewers do the work of finding them.

Here is a structure that removes that work.

1. Decide before you write

Go through every comment and decide what you're doing about it before drafting a single response. There are only three honest options:

  • Accept: you agree and you made the change.
  • Partial: you accept the concern but made a narrower change than asked.
  • Pushback: you respectfully disagree, and you'll explain why.

Drafting before deciding is how letters end up vague. "We have addressed this concern" usually means the author hadn't yet worked out whether they had.

2. Number everything, and split bundled comments

Give every distinct point an ID: R1.1, R1.2, R2.1. If one paragraph from Reviewer 2 contains three requests, it gets three IDs. Reviewers notice when the second half of their comment was silently dropped, and the numbering makes that impossible.

3. Quote, then respond

For each ID, quote the comment, then answer it. Every response does three things:

  1. Acknowledge the point in one sentence. Not flattery. Show you understood it.
  2. Say what you did, specifically. "We added a paragraph to Section 2.3 describing how controls were matched" beats "we clarified the methods."
  3. Say where. Section, paragraph, line or figure. If the reviewer has to hunt, you've lost them.

4. Push back with evidence, not adjectives

Disagreeing is fine; reviewers are often wrong. What doesn't work is disagreeing loudly. A pushback that works:

  • Restates the reviewer's concern fairly, so they can see you heard it.
  • Gives the reason: your design, the literature, or the logic of the analysis.
  • Offers something if you can, such as a sensitivity analysis or a sentence acknowledging the limitation.

Compare "The reviewer is mistaken; random-effects models handle heterogeneity" with "We agree that heterogeneity is high (I² = 78%). We used a random-effects model, which is designed for this situation, and we now report prediction intervals alongside the pooled estimate so readers can see the spread." Same position. Only one of them gets accepted.

(The numbers in that example are illustrative. Use your own.)

5. Never write a change you didn't make

This is the one that matters most, and it's the easiest to get wrong when you're tired or using an AI tool. If the letter says you added a supplementary table, the table must exist. Reviewers check. A single invented change can cost you the trust that every other response depends on.

If you haven't decided or haven't made a change yet, leave a visible placeholder like [describe the matching criteria you used] and fill it in before you submit. Never fill the gap with something that merely sounds right.

6. Open short, close with a summary

Open with three sentences: thank the reviewers, say how many comments you addressed, and say the manuscript has been revised in response. Close with a bulleted list of the major changes, one line each. Editors often read the summary first.

A template

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

Thank you for the careful reviews. We have addressed all [N] comments below; changes in the manuscript are marked in [colour / tracked changes].

Reviewer 1

Comment R1.1: "[quoted comment]"

Classification: Accept

Response: [One-sentence acknowledgement.] [What you changed.] [Where.]

Summary of major changes

  • [One line per change]
