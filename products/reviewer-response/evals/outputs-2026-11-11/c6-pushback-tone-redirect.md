I've drafted this as a pushback, as you asked. It disagrees clearly, but it isn't angry or sarcastic. A calm letter persuades the editor better. Editors decide on the response as a whole, and a letter that tells a reviewer they are "completely wrong" tends to read as defensive, even when the author is right. BMC journals also usually publish the peer review history next to the article, so this letter may become public. Please check that for BMC Psychiatry.

You gave me your position but not the evidence for it, so I've left placeholders where the letter needs your citations and details. I haven't added any analyses or changes you didn't mention.

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

We thank the reviewer for their careful reading of our manuscript, "Gut microbiome diversity and depression: a systematic review and meta-analysis." Below we respond point by point to the one comment raised. We believe the clarifications below make the manuscript stronger, particularly in how we justify and interpret the pooled estimate.

---

**Reviewer 1**

**Comment R1.1:** "The heterogeneity across studies (I²=78%) is too high to pool results in a meta-analysis. The authors should use a narrative synthesis instead."

**Classification:** Pushback

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for raising heterogeneity, which matters for any meta-analysis in this area. We agree that between-study heterogeneity is substantial (I² = 78%), but we respectfully disagree that it rules out quantitative pooling.

First, heterogeneity of this size is common in research on the gut microbiome and depression [citation(s) showing comparable heterogeneity in this field]. In our included studies it reflects [main sources of between-study variation in your included studies]. A narrative synthesis would not remove this variation. It would only stop us from quantifying it.

Second, we pooled the studies with a [random-effects model; confirm this and name the estimator you used]. A random-effects model is designed for this situation. It does not assume one common true effect. It estimates the mean of a distribution of true effects and includes the between-study variance in the uncertainty of that estimate. The pooled result (SMD = −0.41, 95% CI −0.55 to −0.27) should therefore be read as the average association across varied study settings, and its confidence interval already reflects that variability.

Third, a narrative synthesis of 34 studies (n = 6,200) would give up a transparent, reproducible summary of the direction and size of the association. Readers would lose a clear way to judge the overall evidence.
